Adding passenger ships: what the 2030 shore-power rule would cover

From 2030, FuelEU Maritime requires container and passenger ships above 5,000 GT to use shore power when they stay more than two hours at berth in the main EU ports, and the Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation requires those ports to provide it. Adding ferries, ro-pax and cruise ships to the inventory shows how much of the at-berth CO2 that rule would reach: about in Finland and in the 19 Norwegian ports.

At-berth CO2 covered by the 2030 obligation

Share of auxiliary-engine CO2 at berth, cargo and passenger ships, 2019 to 2025

Preliminary. Passenger ships use the at-berth auxiliary power of the Fourth IMO GHG Study by gross tonnage (ro-pax, cruise, passenger ferry) and stays of 0.1 to 240 h. The rule is applied to container and passenger ships above 5,000 GT staying more than two hours, in ports above the AFIR call thresholds (more than 100 container, 40 ro-pax or 25 other passenger calls a year); TEN-T status and the exemptions were not checked. Norway is not an EU member, so its bar shows the rule as if it applied. Passenger ships above 10,000 GT without a car deck are treated as cruise ships, which includes the Norwegian coastal route; battery-electric ferries are not distinguished. The Norwegian register covers local passenger vessels unevenly across years.

What the two countries show

The same method gives two different stories. In Finland cargo calls fell 28% between 2019 and 2025 but CO2 fell only 9%, because each call grew longer and ships grew larger; berths hold half of the total, and the oil berth at Sköldvik alone holds 12%. In Norway CO2 fell 6% as calls declined, Mongstad alone accounts for almost a third of the 19-port total, and the near-zero-carbon grid makes shore power remove 97 to 99% of auxiliary-engine CO2. In both, liquid bulk and general cargo dominate the cargo total, which the 2030 obligation does not reach.

Brazil for comparison, same method: at Porto do Itaqui CO2 rose 31% over 2020 to 2025, driven by longer berth time; three berths held 63%; shore power would cut 84% at a grid factor of 91 g CO2/kWh.

Method and limits

CO2 = auxiliary power at berth (IMO Fourth GHG Study, by segment and DWT class) × hours alongside × SFOC (185 g/kWh, MGO) × 3.206 g CO2/g fuel. Auxiliary boilers are excluded, so totals are a lower bound for tankers. Stays shorter than 1 h or longer than 240 h are removed as recording artefacts or lay-ups. Stays that the Finnish register records at anchorage (port area "ANK") are not berth calls and are excluded. Shore-power scenarios use each year's national grid factor (Statistics Finland table 14qt; NVE climate declaration, Norway) and 97% transmission efficiency; the Finnish factor for 2025 is not yet published, so 2025 uses the 2024 value; they are a technical ceiling, not a forecast. Finnish DWT missing in the register (about % of calls) was imputed from gross tonnage by segment. The main inventory covers cargo ships; passenger ships enter only in the section on the 2030 obligation, and offshore, fishing and service vessels are outside both.

Data table

Data

Finland: Fintraffic Digitraffic port-call API (CC BY 4.0), 219,410 calls with IMO, berth, ATA and ATD; vessel register with GT, DWT and engine power. Norway: Kystverket Kystdatahuset API (open), 211,965 calls in 19 ports with GT and DWT. Downloaded 9 November 2026; passenger extension and audit of the series on 10 November 2026. Code and derived tables on request; the method is the one described in the Itaqui paper and in the open Python library maritimeco2.
