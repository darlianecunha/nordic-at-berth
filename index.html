What the two countries show

The same method gives two different stories. In Finland cargo calls fell 27% between 2019 and 2025 but CO2 fell only 9%, because each call grew longer and ships grew larger; 28 of 414 berths hold half of the total, and the oil berth at Sköldvik alone holds 11%. In Norway CO2 fell 6% as calls declined, Mongstad alone accounts for a third of the 19-port total, and the near-zero-carbon grid makes shore power remove 97 to 99% of auxiliary-engine CO2. In both, liquid bulk and general cargo dominate and the 2030 FuelEU obligation (container and passenger ships) touches only part of the problem.

Brazil for comparison, same method: at Porto do Itaqui CO2 rose 31% over 2020 to 2025, driven by longer berth time; three berths held 63%; shore power would cut 84% at a grid factor of 91 g CO2/kWh.

Method and limits

CO2 = auxiliary power at berth (IMO Fourth GHG Study, by segment and DWT class) × hours alongside × SFOC (185 g/kWh, MGO) × 3.206 g CO2/g fuel. Auxiliary boilers are excluded, so totals are a lower bound for tankers. Stays shorter than 1 h or longer than 240 h are removed as AIS artefacts or lay-ups. Shore-power scenarios use each year's national grid factor (Statistics Finland table 14qt; NVE climate declaration, Norway) and 97% transmission efficiency; they are a technical ceiling, not a forecast. Finnish DWT missing in the register (15% of calls) was imputed from gross tonnage by segment. Passenger ships, ferries, offshore and fishing vessels are outside the inventory.

Data table

Data

Finland: Fintraffic Digitraffic port-call API (CC BY 4.0), 219,410 calls with IMO, berth, ATA and ATD; vessel register with GT, DWT and engine power. Norway: Kystverket Kystdatahuset API (open), 211,965 calls in 19 ports with GT and DWT. Downloaded 9 November 2026. Code and derived tables on request; the method is the one described in the Itaqui paper and in the open Python library maritimeco2.
